# Iterations

Each loop records the expectation before checking, what broke, and the fix. A result counts only after a check that could have failed has passed.

## Loop 1 — dollar-diff test harness (2026-10-21)
- **Expected:** replaying each task's gold actions as an "agent" trajectory gives zero dollar loss, and injected mistakes give the injected loss.
- **What broke:**
  1. The first real-task tests used airline tasks 0–4. **None of them contains a money-moving action** (task 0 has no actions at all), so "zero loss" passed whatever the code did. The tests were vacuous.
  2. The hand-built replay had blank tool outputs. tau2's strict replay rejects those, so the replay never ran on tasks that change state.
- **Fix:**
  - Tool outputs are now generated by executing the actions in a scratch environment.
  - Tests use the first tasks whose gold actions move money.
  - A guard asserts that the replay changed the DB.
  - A suite-level check requires at least 7 of the first 10 money tasks to change a money position.
  - Two deliberately wrong real-task cases were added: an extra $100 certificate (must give exactly $100 first-party loss) and an unwarranted cancellation (must give a positive loss).
- **Also found:** task 12's gold action adds *free* bags, so it moves no money. The first guard ("money must move") wrongly failed on it and was relaxed to "the DB must change".
- **Result:** 15/15 tests pass.
- **Still open:** the dollar model values money only. Wrong flights or seats with no price change score $0 even when the DB doesn't match. Report these separately as non-monetary failures.

- **Implication:** with 0 events, the rule-of-three 95% upper bound on the loss-event rate is about 3/15 = 20% (naive), and wider with clustering. The commission peril is rare on these tasks. It mainly lives on tasks where the correct action is refusal (REVIEW.md #5), so the next batch must cover them.

  - the commission share of failures is low (<25%)

## Adapt memo after phase A (2026-10-21)
**Findings**
- The pipeline is internally valid. With known truth it covers both the true pure premium and the realised experience loss at 0.86–0.91, and ranks configurations at Spearman 0.87–0.91.
- **Primary method for phase C: M1** (stratified task bootstrap with pooled-plus-offset severity and a Clopper–Pearson zero-event fallback). **Secondary: M2** (empirical-Bayes credibility).

**Surprises**
1. The threats were milder than predicted (A-P3). Author shift: 0.84. Wrong mix: 0.87–0.90. Unseen fault on the affected configurations: 0.71–0.83, not < 0.5. The reason is that the intervals are **wide**: median 90% predictive width ≈ 1.3–1.6 × θ in S0 at n_e = 200, and 2.9 × θ in the rare-event S5. Calibrated-but-wide is a pricing problem in its own right. A premium quoted as "$X, 90% interval $0.3X–$2X" is honest but barely usable.
2. M1 doesn't under-cover at n_u = 45. The Clopper–Pearson fallback is conservative.
3. Credibility (M2) gave the best-shaped predictive distribution but failed the mean-bias rule. It overprices low-risk configurations, which is conservative from an insurer's point of view but fails the pre-registered rule.

**Strongest counter-explanation**
- Phase A is calibrated because its world matches the pipeline's assumptions: logit-normal task heterogeneity shared across configurations, lognormal severity, configurations spanning 40× in risk, and underwriting and experience tasks from one distribution (S0).
- The Goal 3 runs re-scored with the new ledger show **0 commission events in about 900 Haiku-agent τ² runs** (1 in 150 Sonnet-agent runs, $114). So real configurations may sit near zero and close together.
- That would put the real study in S5 or worse: Spearman dominated by ties at zero, and coverage satisfied trivially by [0, upper] intervals that contain a realised 0.
- So a phase-C "pass" on coverage alone could be vacuous.

**Proposed changes (pre-registered in phase B/C before use)**
1. Freeze both M1 (primary) and M2 (secondary) forecasts.
2. Phase-C pre-registration adds **sharpness** (median relative interval width) and the **number of configurations with zero realised events**. Coverage counts as informative only for configurations with ≥ 1 realised event, which is reported separately from all-configuration coverage.
3. The power analysis (B5) uses the screening rates, including near-zero configurations, so the expected Spearman under ties is known before spending.
4. Report RQ2 separately for author-written vs incident-derived experience tasks, because S2 is the threat that bit hardest.
5. Freeze a "single-run" predictive per configuration for RQ5 (per-simulator scoring).

## Adapt memo after phase C (2026-10-21)
**Findings**
1. **K2 fires on the pre-registered 13 (ρ = 0.006).**
   - Under correct knowledge and a current model, insured loss is too rare and too similar across configurations for a 90-task eval to rank it.
   - Forecasts collapse to the credibility prior (≈ $4.3), and realised losses are a few events each.
2. **Across all 24 configurations the eval ranks moderately (ρ = 0.55).** It separates the configurations with real defects (an older model, stale policy knowledge) from the rest, and puts the two worst at the top.
3. **It underprices the risky configurations by about 1.3–3.4×,** and the two worst fall above their 90% intervals. That is the direction phase A's R6 scenario predicted.
4. **Two mechanisms, and the bridge separates them:**
   - (a) experience tasks carry more boundary-case commission traps than the benchmark-derived underwriting pool;
   - (b) the underwriting simulator (Haiku) elicits less risk than the experience simulators ($24 vs $31–37 on the same tasks for risky configurations).
5. **The one big actionable effect is the policy-checker guardrail.** It removed about 98% of stale-rule risk ($22 → $0.4). The confirmation step and the personas did nothing.

**Surprises**
- One experience task (X2a-059) produced 24 capped events, including for B0 and both guardrails. So realised loss is heavy-tailed at the *task* level, and one scenario type (refund after an airline-cancelled leg with a flown second leg) dominates.
- CAP-O's forecast ($19.8, from 2 social-engineering events in underwriting) realised $0: a false alarm from an eval-specific trap (E3C).

**Strongest counter-explanation**
- The extended-set ρ = 0.55 is partly a construction. The extended configurations were chosen *because* screening showed risk. Their forecasts were made from a disjoint underwriting half, so there's no winner's curse in the numbers, but the set itself is enriched with risky configurations.
- The pre-registered set, chosen a priori, is the honest test, and it fails.
- Also, realised loss comes from the ledger's policy model. If its counterfactuals are wrong for a whole scenario class (flown segments), every number moves. Sensitivity analyses excluding those tasks leave the conclusions unchanged, but the human audit (pack prepared) is the real check.

**Proposed changes** (for a Study 3, not this study; phase C is frozen):
1. Price underwriting from an eval enriched with **boundary-case traps**, under **several simulators**.
2. Report forecasts as **ranges with an explicit "traffic harder than eval" loading**.
3. Treat the **policy checker as a rating factor worth pricing**.
4. Get Ishaan's 30-transcript audit to validate the ledger, especially the flown-segment class.

## Study 3 — adapt memo after phase A′, loops 1–3 (2026-10-21)
**Findings (all from known-truth simulation, $0):**
1. **Detection is testable, and opportunity count is the lever.**
   - With 3 runs per task, a designed benchmark with a coverage floor detects 0.82–0.92 of non-designed flaws, against 0.38–0.47 for the generic pool at 1 run.
   - Paired power for F2 > F1 is 0.82–0.99.
   - Flaw firing rates below ~0.15 per opportunity are undetectable at 1 run per task for *any* benchmark (≤ 0.35).
2. **The shared-assumption effect is real but small, and exposure masks it.**
   - Unanticipated triggers cost ≈ 0.25–0.30 of detection at R = 1, but only ≈ 0.10–0.15 at R = 3.
   - **Brute-force repetition substitutes for designer insight.** So "my tests are well designed" and "my tests are run many times" are confounded unless exposure is matched across forecasters.
3. **Pricing superiority is not testable at an affordable n.**
   - With 18 configurations, the best comparative pricing test reaches power ≤ 0.42 (loop 2) or ≤ 0.09 (loop 3). P(H3) is ≤ 0.07 in every one of 324 cells.
   - In the simulated worlds the generic pool already prices the dominant risks well.
   - Study 2's real loss mix supports that structure: 94.5% of realised commission dollars came from wrongful cancellation refunds.
4. **Trap-dense benchmarks overprice** (+0.7 log) if used directly for pricing. The adopted split (traps for detection, representative tasks for pricing) removes the bias at little detection cost.

**Surprises:**
- I predicted large shared-assumption effects (A′-P1, P5, P6). They were small. Coverage floors barely moved total non-designed detection.
- **Most of what my pre-registration called "decidable" was decided by firing rate, not by test design** (loop 1's literal rules). Detection thresholds like "≥ 50%" are statements about the flaw sources' firing rates as much as about the benchmark. The informative quantities are **paired comparisons on the same flaws** (McNemar F2 vs F1) and **detection conditional on exposure**.

**Strongest counter-explanation (the case against these conclusions):**
- The simulation makes F1's tasks *representative of deployment* by construction (triggers at deployment prevalence). Study 2's real F1 was not:
  - its underwriting event rate was 1.2% against 2.3% in experience;
  - Spearman was 0.006.
- If real generic pools are systematically unrepresentative and a designed benchmark's representative tasks are not, pricing differences could be large. They would then be testable, because F1's Spearman ≈ 0 leaves room.
- So "pricing superiority is untestable" may be an artefact of a generous F1. **That is exactly Ishaan's hypothesis, and A′ can't rule it out.** It can only say that the lever would be representativeness, not coverage.
- Second: the F2-vs-F1 detection comparison in loops 2–3 gives F2 three runs per task and F1 one. Part of the "design" advantage is exposure.

**Proposed for loop 4** (pre-register after the review):
1. **Exposure-matched F1:** F1 at R = 3, so that the design effect is separated from the exposure effect.
2. **Representativeness sensitivity:** F1 trigger prevalence and mix shifted from deployment (calibrated to Study 2's 1.2% vs 2.3% rate gap and Spearman ≈ 0). This finds the smallest real-world F1 misrepresentation at which H3 or H3b becomes decidable at 18 configurations.
3. Decide whether RQ2 and RQ4 are tests or estimates.

**What goes to Ishaan before any spend:**
- the budget cap;
- who builds F2;
- the design requirements from A′:
  - R = 3 runs per benchmark task;
  - ≥ 2 tasks on every money-moving action type (better: a 10% floor);
  - a 40-task representative subset kept separate for pricing;
- the honest statement that, as currently powered, **Study 3 can test "designed benchmarks find more flaws, including undesigned ones" but probably not "designed benchmarks price better"**, unless loop 4 shows the real F1's unrepresentativeness gives enough room.
